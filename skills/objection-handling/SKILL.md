# Objection handling

Goal: a playbook that helps sellers find the real concern behind an objection and answer it honestly, at every stage of the deal. It covers the objections buyers actually raise, what each one usually means, the questions that uncover it, short responses by root cause, proof, how to prevent it, and drills to practise.

Built on current practice:
- **Diagnose before you answer.** Gong's analysis of 67,149 recorded sales meetings found top performers pause about five times longer after an objection, answer with a question far more often, keep their pace steady, and avoid long monologues. The best answer is usually a better question.
- **LAER** (Carew International): Listen, Acknowledge, Explore, Respond. This skill adds **Pause** before and **Confirm** after: Pause → Listen → Acknowledge → Explore → Respond → Confirm.
- **The stated objection is often a label.** "Too expensive" may mean "I can't justify it to finance" (authority), "I don't trust it will work" (risk), or "I don't see enough value" (need).
- **Indecision is its own problem** (The JOLT Effect, Dixon and McKenna, from 2.5 million sales conversations): 40–60% of qualified deals are lost to no decision, and most of those buyers wanted to change but feared choosing wrong. Status quo needs the cost of inaction made clear; indecision needs the fear of a wrong choice made smaller. JOLT: Judge the level of indecision, Offer a recommendation, Limit exploration, Take risk off the table.
- **Buyers research alone first** (Gartner, 2025–26: most B2B buyers prefer a rep-free experience). Many objections are formed before the first call, so prevention through content and consistent messaging matters as much as live answers.

## 1. Brief (ask only what's missing, max 4 questions, in one message)
Inputs:
- **Company and product** (website, deck, or the positioning, messaging house and battlecards if they exist)
- **Industry and segment** (optional). If named, apply the industry lens in step 3. If not, stay industry-neutral and say so.
- **Buyer roles** in the deal (or an ICP and persona board)
- **Known objections**: from call notes or recordings, CRM loss reasons, lost-deal emails, RFP questions, support tickets. Ask for these once; real objections beat guessed ones.
- **A single objection** if the person only needs help with one: then skip to step 5 for that one and answer it fully.

If nobody is there to answer, make reasonable assumptions, list them at the top, and continue.

## 2. Collect the objections
- From internal sources first (above), quoted in the buyer's words.
- From outside: review sites and forums for the category (complaints and hesitations about similar products), competitor comparison pages, analyst and trade press, community threads, the company's own FAQ and support pages.
- Group duplicates. Keep the 15–25 that matter most; rank them by how often they come up and how many deals they cost (mark the ranking as a hypothesis if there is no data).

## 3. Industry lens (only when an industry is named)
Add the objections and constraints that industry brings. Examples:
- **Regulated (pharma, medical, food supplements, finance)**: "Is it approved / compliant here?", "What can we claim?", documentation, audits. Responses never promise outcomes or claims the product isn't approved for.
- **Manufacturing, chemicals, ingredients**: qualification time, specification fit, switching cost of re-validation, supply security, minimum order, lead time.
- **Software and SaaS**: security review, integration effort, data migration, adoption, contract length, vendor viability.
- **Professional services**: "We can do it in-house", proof of results, team continuity, scope creep.
- **Public sector**: procurement rules, budget cycles, tender requirements, references.
- **Consumer brands selling to retail or distributors**: margin, sell-through, listing fees, promotional support.

State the lens at the top: "Industry lens: [industry]" or "Industry-neutral".

## 4. Categorise and map to the deal
Put each objection in one category:
1. **Need / value**: "We don't need this", "We're fine as we are"
2. **Urgency / timing**: "Not now", "Next quarter", "Send me something"
3. **Price / budget**: "Too expensive", "No budget", "Cheaper elsewhere"
4. **Authority / process**: "I need to check with…", "Procurement decides"
5. **Trust / risk**: "Will it work for us?", "You're too small", "Implementation looks hard"
6. **Competition / status quo**: "We use X", "We'll build it ourselves"
7. **Indecision**: "Let me think about it", "We need to evaluate more options", repeated requests for more information
8. **Brush-offs** (early, reflexive): "Not interested", "Just email me"

Map each to the stage where it appears: first contact, discovery, demo or evaluation, proposal and negotiation, after signature (renewal, expansion).

## 5. Build each objection card
For every objection:
- **What they say**: 2–3 common phrasings, in the buyer's words
- **What it usually means**: 2–3 possible root causes (true objection vs. smokescreen)
- **Explore questions**: 2–3 open, neutral questions to find the real concern (avoid "Why?", which sounds like a challenge; use "What makes you say that?", "Compared to what?", "What would need to be true…?")
- **Respond, by root cause**: one short response per root cause, 3 sentences or fewer, in plain words a seller can say. Acknowledge first, without arguing or conceding.
- **Proof to show**: a case study, number, reference, guarantee, demo moment or document (sourced, or a visible gap like [Customer result needed])
- **Confirm**: one question to check the concern is resolved ("Does that answer it, or is there more behind it?")
- **Isolate** when useful: "Apart from that, is anything else holding you back?"
- **Prevent it**: where to answer it before the buyer raises it (website page, discovery question, demo step, proposal section, comparison sheet)
- **Walk away if**: the signal that this isn't a fit (honest disqualification is a good outcome)

Special cases:
- **Indecision**: use JOLT. Judge how indecisive the buyer is; offer a clear recommendation instead of more options; limit further exploration and information; take risk off the table (pilot, phased start, guarantee, exit clause, references from similar buyers).
- **Status quo**: make the cost of doing nothing concrete in the buyer's numbers, without fear-mongering.
- **Price**: never discount before exploring; move from price to value and total cost; trade, don't give (any concession gets something back, such as term, volume or a case study).
- **Authority**: offer to equip the champion (a short business case, a one-pager for the approver) or to meet the approver together.

## 6. Live method (the strip every seller learns)
**Pause** (2–3 seconds, keep your pace) → **Listen** (let them finish) → **Acknowledge** (show you heard it, without agreeing or arguing) → **Explore** (ask, don't answer) → **Respond** (short, to the real concern) → **Confirm** (check it landed).
Don'ts: interrupting, long monologues, speeding up, arguing, discounting first, answering the stated objection when the real one is different, false urgency or fake deadlines.

## 7. Practise
- **Top 10 drill**: the 10 most costly objections, practised in pairs: one plays the buyer (using the root-cause variants), one the seller.
- **Scoring rubric** (1–5 each): paused, explored before answering, answered the real concern, used proof, confirmed, stayed short and calm.
- **AI role-play prompt**: a ready prompt the seller can paste into Claude to rehearse: Claude plays a named buyer role with a hidden root cause and scores the seller against the rubric.

## 8. Write it well
- Human and respectful. The goal is to help the buyer decide well, not to "win the argument".
- Short sentences a seller can say out loud. No scripts that sound like scripts.
- No buzzwords, no manipulation tactics, no false scarcity, no pressure closes.
- Never invent customers, numbers, quotes or guarantees. Unknowns become visible gaps.

## 9. Visualize
Use a Design or Dashboard artifact type if listed and fitting; otherwise one HTML artifact. Brand colours of the client if known, otherwise Byzantine purple (#702963), near-black (#1F1A1E), light tint (#F6F1F5) and Montserrat. Flat and minimal, no stock images or emoji.
- **Method strip** across the top: Pause → Listen → Acknowledge → Explore → Respond → Confirm, each with one line.
- **Objection map**: a grid of categories (rows) × deal stages (columns); each cell shows the objections that appear there, sized or shaded by how often or how costly. Click an objection to jump to its card.
- **Objection cards**: grouped by category, each with what they say, what it means, explore questions, responses by root cause, proof, confirm, prevent. A "Copy" button on each response.
- **Top 10 cheat sheet**: one printable page with the 10 most costly objections, one explore question and one response each.
- **Practice section**: the drill, the rubric and the AI role-play prompt with a copy button.

## 10. Check before showing
- [ ] Objections come from real sources where possible, quoted in the buyer's words; guesses are marked
- [ ] Every card has explore questions before responses
- [ ] Responses are 3 sentences or fewer and answer a specific root cause
- [ ] Indecision and status quo are handled differently
- [ ] Every proof point is sourced or shown as a gap
- [ ] Each objection has a prevention step
- [ ] Industry lens applied if named; otherwise marked industry-neutral
- [ ] No manipulative tactics, false urgency or invented claims

## 11. Handoff
In the reply: the 5 most costly objections and the real concern usually behind each, the biggest prevention fix (what to change in the website, pitch or demo so the objection comes up less), and the proof still missing. Suggest measuring: objection frequency by stage, conversion after the objection, and deals lost to no decision. Offer one next step: battlecards per competitor, a role-play session using the AI prompt, or a buyer-facing FAQ page that answers the top objections before the first call.
